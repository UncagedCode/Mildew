# 04 — Launch Game Specifications

This document defines the launch 6+2 roster. Each game must be fun without any sinister incidents. All eight games must support two real players through the adaptations below.

---

# 1. Guess the Genitals

## Role

Flagship gross visual trivia and one of the clearest statements of Mildew's identity.

## Content rule

Animal genitalia only. No human sexual anatomy in the core game. Use real zoological photography where suitable and properly licensed; accurate illustration is acceptable where photography is impractical or inappropriate. Presentation is clinical/factual, not pornographic.

## Standard loop

1. TV shows a cropped/controlled image.
2. Graham invites players to inspect it with complete professionalism.
3. Phones show candidate animals.
4. Players choose one answer.
5. Answer locks.
6. TV reveals the species and wider context/illustration.
7. Graham delivers a short genuine biological fact.
8. Score and possible Rot/callbacks are applied.

Typical game length: 5–7 minutes.
Typical standard game: ~7 questions.

Suggested pacing:

- Q1–2: relatively accessible;
- Q3–5: stranger;
- Q6: particularly bizarre;
- Q7: `FINAL GENITAL`, enhanced value.

## Scoring

Baseline:

- Correct: 1,000
- Speed bonus: up to ~150, capped; accuracy remains dominant
- Hard/themed/final: 1,250–1,500
- Wrong: 0
- Streak: small bonus only
- Fast correct identification of particularly gross/obscure material may add Rot and Graham commentary

No standard negative points.

## Variants

### Whose Is It?
Standard species identification.

### Match the Member
Multiple animal names and multiple diagrams/images; match them.

### Functional Genitals
Identify what a bizarre reproductive structure does.

### Genital or Something Else?
Some images are mushrooms, flowers, sea creatures, fruit, geological structures, etc. Use occasionally so players cannot assume every image is genitalia.

### Themed Round
Possible themes:

- mammals;
- birds;
- marine animals;
- reptiles;
- invertebrates;
- extreme reproductive adaptations.

### Confidence/Wager Variant
Occasional advanced round. Players may wager a limited amount (e.g. 0–500) or choose a confidence option before reveal. Never make this the default loop.

## Two-player behaviour

Works largely unchanged. In close scores, use a short `GENITAL TIE-BREAKER` if required.

## Graham behaviour hooks

- exceptionally fast correct answer: `1.2 seconds, Steven. Anything you'd like to tell us?`
- long correct streak: praise + Rot;
- repeated wrong animal: recurring label/callback;
- player continually chooses horse: `Aaron's gone with horse again.`
- themed punishment: Graham can insert a gross/extended biology round if the group has irritated him.

## Interference hooks

Keep normally light because the premise itself is already absurd.

Possible rare phone message:

`STOP LOOKING AT IT`

Possible rare inappropriate studio callback during reveal.

## Content targets

Serious PoC: 15–20 curated items.
Long-term: 60–100+ approved items.

Every factual item carries source/citation metadata and image licensing metadata.

# 2. Hole

## Role

Progressive visual deduction with risk/reward; simple enough to understand instantly but mechanically more interesting than plain multiple choice.

## Core loop

A highly cropped image of a hole/opening appears. It can belong to:

- an animal;
- anatomy;
- machinery;
- architecture;
- geology;
- nature;
- household object;
- drain/pipe;
- burrow;
- shell;
- cave;
- etc.

The image reveals progressively.

At each reveal, players may **LOCK IN** a guess/category. Earlier lock = more points. Once locked, it is locked.

Possible structure:

1. extreme close-up;
2. partial zoom-out;
3. broader context;
4. final multiple-choice safety net.

## Scoring

Suggested baseline:

- Lock after first reveal and correct: 1,500
- Second reveal: 1,100
- Third reveal: 750
- Final multiple choice: 500
- Wrong early lock: 0
- Optional small partial credit when broad category is correct but specific identity is wrong

No standard negative points.

## Variants

### Double Hole
Two holes; determine which belong to the same object/species/category.

### Scale
Guess whether the opening is microscopic, centimetres wide, human-sized or enormous.

### Which End?
Intentionally juvenile title; identify which end/part of an animal/object the image represents.

### Advanced Open Guess
High familiarity tier: some rounds omit the final multiple-choice safety net or ask typed/category guesses.

### Studio Hole
Very rare content/incident crossover where a mystery opening is eventually revealed to be a real part of the Mildew studio/backstage world. Never explain why it is in the question library.

## Two-player behaviour

Works unchanged and makes a strong head-to-head game.

## Interference hook

Wrong-camera cut to an actual dark doorway or service opening, followed by Graham saying:

> “Not that one.”

Then return immediately to the next normal Hole.

## Content targets

Serious PoC: 20–30.
Long-term: 80–120+.

# 3. Mildew Survey

## Role

Primary social writing/voting game. Very high replayability because players generate most of the comedy.

## Core loop

1. Graham presents a question.
2. Players privately type answers.
3. Answers appear anonymously.
4. Depending on round format, players vote/guess/match.
5. Results reveal authors and/or archive status.
6. Graham comments.

Example prompts:

- `What's the worst thing to discover underneath a hotel mattress?`
- `What's something you absolutely shouldn't lick?`
- `What's the least reassuring thing a surgeon can say?`
- `What shouldn't make a crunching sound?`

## Self-voting

Players normally **cannot vote for themselves**. Special rounds may explicitly permit selfishness.

## Archived Responses

The Mildew archive supplies authored filler responses, particularly for two-player games.

Archive responses may be:

- normal;
- funny;
- oddly specific;
- allegedly from recurring historic contestants;
- old local player responses reused later with no direct attribution.

This is both a low-player-count solution and worldbuilding mechanism.

## Round formats

### Popularity
Vote favourite/best response.

### Match
Bonus when independent players produce equivalent/simple matching concepts.

Use local normalization/keyword matching for simple matches such as `dog` vs `a dog`. Do not build a cloud-AI dependency for semantic equivalence. Complex equivalents may remain separate unless a robust offline solution is introduced later.

### Who Said That?
Guess the author.

### Archive
Identify which answer is allegedly from Mildew's archive.

### None of These
Selected prompts may allow a `none of these` vote.

## Scoring

- Votes received = main points
- Independent concept match = bonus
- Archive answers cannot earn player points
- Occasional small `Presenter's Bonus` from Graham
- Wrong votes normally do not subtract

## Two-player behaviour

Use two player answers plus 2–4 archive responses as needed. Ensure there is enough material for a meaningful vote/guess.

## Rare manipulation

Graham may very rarely alter/display an answer differently for punishment or character comedy.

Player typed:

> `a rat`

TV displays:

> `GRAHAM MILDEW`

Graham:

> “Charming.”

This must be rare enough that players still trust ordinary answer display.

Graham may also rarely discard an answer:

> “No. We're not putting that on television.”

## Persistent reuse

Selected funny player answers may enter the local archive pool for future sessions. Keep this local. Do not pretend it came from the internet.

## Content targets

Serious PoC: 40–60 prompts.
Long-term: 200+.

# 4. Real or Mildew?

## Role

Proper bizarre knowledge/trivia spine. Gives Mildew factual depth.

## Core premise

Several ridiculous claims/images are shown. Depending on the round, players identify the real one or the fabricated one.

Examples of round language:

- `WHICH ONE IS REAL?`
- `THREE ARE REAL. ONE IS MILDEW.`
- rare: `ALL OF THESE ARE REAL`
- rare: `NONE OF THESE ARE REAL`

Fabricated answers must be plausible and written in the **same factual tone** as genuine options. Avoid obvious joke decoys that remove deduction.

## Categories

- medicine;
- biology;
- history;
- food;
- inventions;
- advertising;
- scientific oddities;
- historical customs;
- disasters;
- strange objects/images.

Avoid current/recent identifiable tragedy as comedy material.

## Scoring

- Standard correct: 1,000
- Hard/obscure: ~1,250
- Small speed bonus
- Wrong: 0
- Small streak bonus

## Confidence variant

Occasional option:

- `NORMAL`
- `I'M CERTAIN`

`I'M CERTAIN` may double the question value or offer a strong bonus. A confident wrong answer does not need a direct score penalty but gives Graham excellent humiliation/Rot/targeting material.

> “Certain, were you?”

## Reveal

TV shows a concise factual explanation. Full citations/sources live in the Viewer Information Service/Fact Archive.

## Two-player behaviour

Works unchanged.

## Familiarity

At higher familiarity, use increasingly obscure material. Graham may eventually say:

> “You've had the easy ones.”

## Content targets

Serious PoC: 30–40.
Long-term: 150+.

# 5. Police Sketch

## Role

Drawing/interpretation game; player-generated comedy and communication.

## Drawing tools

Keep intentionally crude:

- pen;
- limited thickness selection;
- eraser;
- undo;
- clear;
- submit.

No complex art suite.

## 4+ player default chain

Example chain:

1. Player A receives a prompt.
2. A draws.
3. Player B sees only drawing, writes what they believe prompt was.
4. Player C sees B's description, redraws.
5. Continue as appropriate for player count/time.
6. Reveal the mutation chain.

Prompt example:

> “A suspicious-looking pigeon wearing something it clearly stole.”

## Two-player adaptation

- Player A draws prompt.
- Player B interprets.
- reveal;
- roles swap with a new prompt.

Optionally compare against a deliberately bad `official police reconstruction` generated/authored by the game.

## Variants

### Witness Description
Players briefly see an image, then reconstruct from memory.

### Body Part
Graham describes unusual anatomy without showing it; player draws what they think he means.

### Reconstruct the Suspect
Different players draw different portions without seeing the full composition.

### Draw What You Saw
Rare Interruption Game variant. Player is told to draw what they saw even though they were never shown anything. Use rarely and do not make it a standard game rule.

## Timing

Typical drawing: 45–60 seconds depending on complexity.

## Scoring

Primarily group voting and chain fidelity:

- closest to original;
- funniest mutation;
- Graham's Choice;
- original artist gains points if later players correctly reconstruct the prompt.

Allow players to vote in multiple categories. Graham's Choice is a modest bonus, not the dominant scoring route.

## Graham commentary

Sparse. Prefer shit-talking rather than constant narration.

Examples:

> “Claire appears to have abandoned perspective entirely.”

> “I'm told that's a dog.”

## Persistent reuse

Rarely reuse old local drawings as props/evidence/background gags in later sessions.

## Content targets

Serious PoC: 30–40 prompts.
Long-term: 150+.

# 6. Do Not Press That

## Role

Real asymmetric cooperative communication puzzle. Must be mechanically satisfying even with no horror layer.

## Core loop

TV presents a problem and/or shared status. Each phone receives different controls and/or instructions.

Example:

TV:

> `RESTORE STUDIO POWER`

Aaron phone:

> RED / BLUE / GREEN switches

Sarah phone:

> `Red may only be activated after two tones.`
>
> `Never mention Blue.`

Claire phone:

> dial 1–8

Sam phone:

> `Claire must set her dial to the number of green lights visible on Aaron's panel.`

Players must communicate verbally and operate their own controls.

## Design rule

Constraints must require genuine coordination, not random button mashing.

## Puzzle length

Roughly 45–90 seconds each.
Typical game: 4–5 puzzles.
Typical total: 7–9 minutes.

## Two-player adaptation

Each player receives more controls and/or more instruction fragments. Puzzles must have explicit 2-player layouts, not simply remove roles and hope the logic works.

## Mistakes

Usually cause **partial consequences**, not instant failure:

- disable a control temporarily;
- change instruction state;
- reduce time;
- add a complication;
- require recovery step;
- lower success tier.

Avoid simply subtracting points from everyone for one mistake.

Graham can publicly identify the culprit:

> “That was Steven.”

Very rarely he can blame the wrong person if targeting them.

## Success tiers

- Perfect
- Completed
- Barely Completed
- Failed Spectacularly

Team receives a shared success score. Individual small bonuses may recognise correct operations, clutch actions or avoiding mistakes, but should not incentivise teammates to compete for controls.

## Irrelevant controls

Some panels may contain controls that should never be touched. Graham may explicitly say not to touch something, naturally tempting people.

## Timer irregularity

Rare incident variant: TV timer reaches zero but puzzle continues because the displayed timer was wrong. The phone/controller must still communicate the real interaction state clearly enough to avoid genuine usability failure.

## Punishment hooks

Graham may add a special control to the person who broke the previous puzzle, or assign them an emergency release.

## PoC target

8–12 strong puzzles.
Long-term: 30–50 puzzle templates/scenarios.

# 7. The Basement

## Role

Longer cooperative discussion/deduction system that can be mundane, gross, sinister, absurd or partially unresolved.

It should broaden Mildew's emotional range without turning into a dedicated horror game.

## Standard loop

1. TV presents a mystery/aftermath.
2. Each player receives private evidence.
3. Group discusses verbally.
4. Limited optional investigations become available.
5. Players individually submit their final theory.
6. Reveal shows the best-supported/canonical explanation where one exists.
7. Score based on theory accuracy/partial support plus small investigation-efficiency bonus.

Example setup:

> `WHAT HAPPENED IN THE BASEMENT?`

Player A:

> `A window is open from the inside.`

Player B:

> `Muddy footprints end beneath a table.`

Player C:

> `A bowl of cereal is still warm.`

## Scenario tone variation

Possible outcomes:

- disturbing;
- gross;
- criminally weird;
- apparently supernatural;
- completely mundane;
- ridiculous.

Players should not know the genre of the current case.

Example mundane reveal:

> A fox entered through a basement window and destroyed a child's birthday cake.

Graham:

> “Poor cake.”

## Answers

Usually there is a definitive best-supported answer. Roughly 10–15% may remain partially unresolved. Even ambiguous scenarios need an internal best-supported scoring model.

Multiple theories can receive partial credit when they capture key facts.

## Private evidence

Some players may receive uniquely important/sensitive evidence, e.g.:

> `You recognise the handwriting.`

The player decides whether to share it verbally. Do not enforce sharing; Graham may comment on obvious silence.

False/unreliable clues may exist, but not every case should contain a liar role.

## Final submission

Players submit **individually**, then the TV shows whether the group agreed. Avoid requiring unanimous consensus that lets one loud player dominate.

## Recurrence

Use recurring fictional locations/people across some cases. This may quietly touch the larger Mildew world, but do not create a clean overarching plot.

## Two-player adaptation

Give each player multiple evidence cards and enough investigation agency to maintain discussion.

## Scenario data structure

Each authored scenario should contain:

- setup;
- private evidence pools;
- player-count allocation rules;
- optional investigation nodes;
- theory options/semantic scoring rules;
- reveal;
- partial-credit mapping;
- tone tags;
- content tags;
- Familiarity tier;
- incident hooks;
- recurring-location/character references;
- factual-source metadata if real claims are used.

## Content target

Serious PoC: 6–8 strong cases.
Long-term: 25–40+.

# 8. Mouthfeel

## Role

Text-heavy gross creative-writing/voting game providing contrast with visual-heavy formats.

Imagery may support the prompt but text remains primary.

## Core format

TV gives an object or combination:

> `cold porridge + carpet underlay`

Players describe the imagined mouthfeel.

Responses appear anonymously and are voted on.

## Round categories

- funniest;
- most disgusting;
- most believable;
- `I can feel this in my mouth`;
- most accurate.

Using multiple voting categories changes player strategy and prevents pure comedy from dominating.

## Reverse format

Graham gives a texture description:

> `Warm, slightly fibrous, initially soft but unexpectedly crunchy.`

Players choose/guess what he is supposedly describing.

## Make It Worse

Collaborative escalation:

- Player 1 receives base item;
- adds a horrible modification;
- next player adds another;
- final object/meal is displayed;
- group votes.

Participants share points based on final reception.

## Horrible Choice bonus

Occasional short bonus:

> Which would you rather eat?

Use as a small variant, not the entire game.

## Graham contribution

Graham can anonymously contribute an authored answer. His answer can genuinely win.

If revealed as winner:

> “Naturally.”

## Archive

Occasionally insert an old local player response from the archive pool.

## Scoring

- votes received = main points;
- Graham's Choice = modest bonus;
- Make It Worse chain members share points;
- selected particularly vile answers may receive Rot;
- wrong/low-vote answers do not lose points.

## Two-player adaptation

Each player submits two answers or one answer plus alternates, supplemented by one or more archive/Graham responses so voting remains meaningful.

## Content target

Serious PoC: 40–60 prompts/components.
Long-term: 200+.

# Cross-game rules

## Typical maximum runtime

- Guess the Genitals: 5–7 min
- Hole: 5–7 min
- Survey: 6–8 min
- Real or Mildew?: 5–7 min
- Police Sketch: 7–9 min
- Do Not Press That: 7–9 min
- Basement: 8–12 min
- Mouthfeel: 6–8 min

Director may shorten future games when the show runs late by reducing remaining rounds, never by abruptly cutting an active meaningful round.

Director may occasionally extend a game, especially when the room is enjoying/failing it or Graham is punishing them.

## Round visibility

Do not routinely show `Question 3/7` unless the format benefits from it. Players should not always know exactly when Mildew will stop a segment.

Phones may show precise timers when gameplay requires them. TV timing can be theatrical, but the real player deadline must remain unambiguous.

## Submission

- multiple-choice answers generally lock when selected where intended;
- Hole early lock cannot be undone;
- text/drawing uses a clear Submit action;
- reconnect should preserve unsent work where practical.

## AFK

If a player does not answer:

- proceed without deadlocking;
- award 0 for that interaction;
- Graham notices repeated inactivity;
- possible line: `Aaron remains with us in a largely ceremonial capacity.`

Graham may punish AFK socially, but do not create a task requiring someone who is clearly away. Example:

> “Aaron has contributed nothing, so we're giving everyone else 200 points.”
