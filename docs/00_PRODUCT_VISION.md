# 00 — Product Vision

## Elevator pitch

**Mildew** is a local-multiplayer adult party game presented as a forgotten British television game show from roughly 1997–1999. One Android TV runs the broadcast; 2–8 people join on their phones over local Wi‑Fi. The games are funny, competitive, gross, social and varied: animal anatomy, visual deduction, surveys, drawing, bizarre factual trivia, cooperative communication puzzles and unsettling deduction scenarios.

The show also behaves incorrectly in ways it refuses to explain. Cameras occasionally cut to the wrong room. Crew audio leaks onto the feed. Graham Mildew develops favourites and grudges. The Announcer occasionally contradicts him. A phone may briefly say `WHY ARE YOU WATCHING THIS` and return immediately to a normal answer screen. A scream can happen somewhere backstage, followed by somebody sharply saying `SHUT UP`, then the show cuts straight back to a cheerful game. These moments are not the game’s primary genre. They are the thing that makes the room stop laughing for three seconds before Mildew immediately gives them another stupid task.

## Core product pillars

### 1. It must be a good party game without the horror layer

If all irregularities are disabled, the group should still want to play. Every launch game has a distinct social/mechanical role. Mildew cannot survive on shock value alone.

### 2. Grossness should often contain real knowledge

Animal anatomy, parasites, strange biology and odd historical/scientific facts should frequently teach something genuine. The best reaction is:

> “That is disgusting. I can't believe that's real.”

The factual layer gives the ridiculous presentation credibility and longevity.

### 3. The broadcast treats everything as normal

Mildew never announces `SCARY MODE`. Graham presents a walrus penis with the same professionalism as a mainstream game-show host presenting a general-knowledge question. A disturbing production incident ends and the programme simply carries on.

### 4. The weirdness is implication, not exposition

The game should never resolve into a conventional plot where the players discover the demon behind Mildew. Players may construct theories. The software does not confirm them.

### 5. Players generate a large portion of the comedy

Survey answers, drawings, explanations, coordination failures, social voting and recurring callbacks are essential. Prepared content should create a stage for the room rather than replacing the room.

### 6. The show remembers just enough to feel alive

Within a session, Mildew remembers repeated choices, failures, successes, disgusting knowledge, recurring answer categories and player dynamics. Across sessions, it remembers names, selected jokes, prizes, familiarity, certain rare incidents and room states. It should sometimes make a returning player feel recognised without becoming a surveillance gimmick.

### 7. The TV/phone split is part of the fiction

The TV is the public broadcast. Phones are private **Mildew Home Response Units**. The TV shows communal information and spectacle. Phones can show hidden instructions, private clues, secret roles and very rare interference that no one else can verify.

## Target audience experience

A strong Mildew session should naturally produce moments like:

- everybody loudly arguing about whether a close-up is a nostril or a drain;
- one player being absurdly good at identifying animal genitalia and being mocked for it;
- two players writing the same horrible Survey answer independently;
- a drawing chain turning a perfectly sensible prompt into an incomprehensible creature;
- the group shouting contradictory instructions during Do Not Press That;
- one player asking, “Did anyone else's phone just say ‘you need to help me’?” and receiving blank looks;
- Graham punishing the group with an extended parasite round because one contestant irritated him;
- a camera cutting to a recurring empty room for one second;
- a commercial for MEAT-O arriving immediately after something genuinely uncomfortable;
- the session ending with fake prizes, personal awards and arguments about whether one of the strange moments was actually part of the game.

## Product format

### Standard Broadcast

Approx. 45 minutes, around five core games selected by the Director, plus:

- opening titles;
- contestant introductions;
- adverts;
- mini-interstitials;
- possible punishments;
- possible Interruption Games;
- midpoint commercial/bathroom break;
- finale;
- personal awards;
- terrible prize;
- credits.

### Quick Broadcast

Approx. 20 minutes, roughly three games and fewer opportunities for deep escalation.

### Full Mildew

Approx. 90+ minutes, allowing familiarity, pressure, recurring-room changes, private interference and long-form callback systems more room to breathe.

### Custom

May exist for testing/player control, but the flagship experience is a Director-selected programme. The point is that **Mildew decides what is on**.

## Launch roster

The initial release/proof of concept centres on eight games:

1. Guess the Genitals
2. Hole
3. Mildew Survey
4. Real or Mildew?
5. Police Sketch
6. Do Not Press That
7. The Basement
8. Mouthfeel

The first six are core launch formats. The Basement and Mouthfeel are the additional ambitious/experimental formats that broaden tone and interaction.

## What Mildew is not

Mildew is not:

- a generic web quiz with a VHS filter;
- a straight Jackbox clone;
- a jumpscare horror game;
- an ARG that requires players to catalogue clues;
- a lore puzzle with a canonical monster reveal;
- an internet-required service;
- a game that secretly listens to the room;
- a system that pretends real device failures are fictional events;
- a game that makes exiting, pausing or adjusting comfort settings difficult;
- a shock-content slideshow with no mechanical variety.

## Success metric

The concept works when a real group can complete a local session, laugh because the games themselves are good, remember specific personal callbacks and failures, experience at least one moment that makes the room genuinely ask “what the fuck was that?”, and immediately want another broadcast because they know the next one will not play out the same way.
