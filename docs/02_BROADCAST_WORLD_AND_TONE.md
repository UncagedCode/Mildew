# 02 — Broadcast World and Tone Bible

## The central fiction

Mildew should feel like a cheap British game show that aired in the late 1990s and legally should have been cancelled, but somehow continues transmitting. The production has an implied past, but it is not a conventional narrative campaign.

The game should constantly preserve ambiguity between:

- incompetence;
- exploitative television production;
- archive corruption;
- deliberate manipulation by Graham;
- conflict within production;
- something impossible happening inside the programme.

Writers and developers must resist the urge to “pay off” this ambiguity with a monster or clean explanation.

## Humour model

The preferred humour is **dead-serious professionalism applied to absurd or disgusting content**.

Good:

> “Aaron has identified the tapir correctly.”
>
> “Very quickly, in fact.”

Good:

> “Claire appears to have abandoned perspective entirely.”

Good:

> “We've temporarily misplaced Sarah.”

Avoid making every Graham line a joke. Normal presenter lines are necessary for the abnormal ones to land.

## Participation guilt

The game should occasionally create the feeling that the players are authorising something they do not understand.

Examples:

- `ALL PARTICIPANTS PRESENT VOLUNTARILY?` with a simple acknowledgement during setup;
- `SOMEONE MUST AUTHORISE THE NEXT SEGMENT` and one player presses a harmless button;
- later callback: `This segment was authorised by Aaron.`
- `RELEASE SPECIMEN? YES / NO`, where no literal real-world harm occurs, but the downstream audio/presentation makes the choice feel suspicious.

Never imply real danger outside the fiction. The emotional goal is complicity in the fictional broadcast.

## Tonal whiplash

After a strong unsettling event, the normal preferred move is not escalating horror. It is something ridiculous.

Example rhythm:

1. Private message: `YOU NEED TO HELP ME`.
2. Brief backstage cutaway and muffled banging.
3. Graham: `Not again.`
4. black frame for a beat.
5. huge trumpet sting.
6. `HOLE!`

The room laughs, then five minutes later somebody remembers the earlier incident.

## Graham's visible emotional system

Unlike hidden Director variables, Graham's state is visible through acting/presentation.

Suggested state set:

- relaxed;
- pleased;
- amused;
- irritated;
- angry;
- embarrassed;
- rattled.

His state may affect line pools and occasionally game/segment choice. It should not mechanically dictate the next event every time; sometimes the Director deliberately does the opposite so players cannot reverse-engineer him.

Examples:

### Relaxed

Smooth presenter patter, ordinary banter, confident timing.

### Pleased

Warmer praise, indulges favourite contestants, may offer a bonus.

### Irritated

Shorter responses, more clipped contestant names, greater punishment eligibility.

### Angry

Rare. Smile disappears. Delivery becomes genuinely sharp. May refuse Announcer instruction, snap at crew or visibly suppress frustration.

### Rattled

He knows something is off but tries to keep presenting. Very valuable because it suggests a boundary even Graham did not expect.

## Player-specific comedy

The show should form lightweight behavioural tags, not real personality analysis. Useful in-session tags include:

- answers unusually quickly;
- often correct;
- often wrong;
- confidently wrong;
- chooses disgusting options;
- presses suspicious buttons;
- risk-averse;
- high Rot;
- drawing favourite;
- weak at coordination;
- often agrees with another player;
- recurring category choice (horse, mollusc, etc.).

Graham may invent an unfair label from very weak evidence:

> **HORSE MAN**

The label may become a running joke. Rarely a false belief persists to another session.

## Favourites and dislikes

Favouritism should create social comedy without corrupting core competitive fairness.

Examples:

> “Very close. Though obviously we're all hoping Sam wins.”

> “Yes, Daniel. We know.”

> Audience boos Sarah.
>
> Graham: “Oh, shut up. It wasn't that bad.”
>
> to Sarah: “It was bad.”

## Punishment segments

These are Director-inserted short segments, not a separate menu game category.

Possible forms:

### The Easy One

Target receives insultingly obvious questions after repeated failure.

### Extra Time

A round appears to end. Graham refuses and adds more.

### Again

Repeat a category/task that someone repeatedly failed.

### Solo Performance

One player receives a brief individual task while everyone watches.

### Apology

Player has 20 seconds to write an apology to Graham. He scores it or rejects it.

### Explain Yourself

Graham calls out a submitted choice and demands a brief defence.

### Re-Education

Fake educational film because the group performed badly.

Example:

> `MILDEW EDUCATIONAL SERVICES PRESENTS: HORSES — WHAT THEY LOOK LIKE`

Punishments may secretly contain large point opportunities. Do not make the reward structure consistent.

## Audience

The audience is mostly heard, not seen.

Audio palette:

- applause of several sizes;
- laughter;
- awkward laughter;
- boos;
- groans;
- gasps;
- murmuring;
- coughing;
- isolated shouts;
- chants;
- chairs/movement.

The audience may:

- react incorrectly;
- applaud before the answer;
- refuse to applaud a player;
- boo someone;
- chant a name;
- contain one conspicuous cough/laugh;
- sound too large for the visible audience;
- become suddenly silent.

Visuals are normally silhouettes or backs of heads. Rarely, visible audience size can conflict with the soundtrack.

## The Announcer

The Announcer is initially a normal utility layer and can slowly become more present. Their writing style should be different from unknown private messages and Graham.

Early:

> “This is Mildew.”

> “Mildew is sponsored tonight by DampAway.”

Later:

> “Graham, we're out of time.”

Later still:

> `ANNOUNCER: DON'T LISTEN TO HIM`

Never confirm whether a phone message that claims to be the Announcer genuinely is.

## Production staff

Maintain a small recurring cast such as:

- floor manager;
- camera operator;
- makeup person;
- technician;
- producer.

Names emerge only through incidental dialogue. They may disappear for long periods. Avoid turning them into RPG-style named NPCs with bios.

## Recurring rooms

Initial world set:

1. Green Room
2. Studio C
3. Service Corridor
4. Prop Store
5. Graham's Dressing Room
6. Archive/Tape Room
7. Loading Area
8. Control Room
9. Staff Kitchenette
10. Unidentified Locked Room

The first serious PoC needs 4–5 of these fully realised.

Rooms have state variants. The state system is persistent but non-linear; a room can become stranger then appear ordinary later.

Examples of small state changes:

- chair moves;
- light left on/off;
- cup appears;
- door open/closed;
- wet patch spreads;
- CRT turns on;
- person glimpsed behind frosted glass;
- somebody sits facing wall;
- room empty again next time.

## Wrong-camera grammar

Players need to learn normal cameras so wrong cameras matter.

Suggested cameras:

- Camera 1: Graham frontal;
- Camera 2: wide studio;
- Camera 3: contestant/podium angle;
- Camera 4: roaming/utility.

Normal cuts may have small live-TV imperfections. Incidents can then use:

- wrong camera for 0.5–2 seconds;
- camera pointed at floor;
- empty corridor;
- camera not ready;
- operator walking out of frame;
- impossible angle;
- backstage still appearing in the wrong context.

## Audio irregularities

Frequency hierarchy:

- production bumps/arguments/cues: reasonably available;
- unexplained sounds: uncommon;
- actual scream: rare;
- scream + sharp `SHUT UP`: very rare major incident.

Other sounds:

- dropped equipment;
- feedback;
- running footsteps;
- phone ringing backstage;
- off-camera argument;
- `we're live`;
- `not now`;
- `who opened that?`;
- somebody crying very faintly;
- laughter from the wrong apparent location;
- Graham's voice faintly audible while visible Graham is silent.

Use deliberate silence as a major tool. Do not score every disturbing moment with horror music.

## Broadcast incidents

The rarest incidents should generate folklore between real players. They are not achievements.

Examples of appropriate *shape*, not mandatory canon:

- after credits, the studio camera returns to an empty set for 20 seconds;
- a different presenter handles exactly one question and nobody acknowledges it;
- audience disappears visually while applause continues;
- a recurring room contains a person who has never been shown before;
- a private message references a room that later appears on TV;
- fragmented private messages combine into a meaning only if players compare notes;
- Graham says `Welcome back` even though the show never left;
- a hidden Interruption Game such as `THE TEST` appears with no menu entry.

Do not overpopulate these early. Rarity creates power.
